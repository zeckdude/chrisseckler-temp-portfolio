# Chris — Core Values & Leadership Philosophy

> These are the principles that guide how Chris works, leads, and what he needs to do his best work. Written to be internalized — useful for interviews, introductions, and conversations about team fit.

---

## Core Value #1: Systems Over Individual Preference

**One-liner:** Consistency is how a whole team gets fast, not just one person.

Chris values **shared patterns and consistency over any individual's personal preference**. When a team agrees on an approach, everyone following that approach is what makes the codebase legible to the next person who touches it, makes bug fixes propagate everywhere instead of needing to be hunted down, and lets new engineers ramp up in days instead of weeks.

**The concrete payoff of consistency:**
1. Anyone who joins can look at any part of the codebase and already understand the shape of it, because it matches everything else.
2. You're not maintaining ten different solutions to the same problem. One pattern, one place to fix it.
3. A fix in one place ripples everywhere instead of requiring five separate patches.
4. Net result: fewer bugs, faster onboarding, faster iteration.

**What this is not:** Rigidity about which pattern wins. Chris is flexible on the "what" — he'll debate approaches. He's firm on "once we've decided, we all do it the same way."

> **In his own words:** "I'm not trying to control how people work. I care about systems because they make the whole team faster. When everyone follows the same pattern, a fix in one place ripples everywhere, bugs are easier to catch, and new people ramp up in days instead of weeks. That upfront discipline pays for itself fast."

## Core Value #2: Mentorship as a Core Responsibility

**One-liner:** Helping people advance is part of the job, not a side activity.

Chris sees mentoring junior and mid-level engineers as one of his responsibilities as a senior — not optional, not extra credit.

### His 3-Step Mentoring Model

**1. Calibrate autonomy to the person.**
How much independence someone gets depends on their confidence level and domain expertise — not a fixed rule. A newer engineer might pair closely with Chris on architecture decisions. A more experienced engineer might design it themselves and bring it to Chris for a gut-check. It's a judgment call made per person, per problem.

**2. Guide without hand-holding.**
Chris is in the room to help people think through hard architecture decisions — not to make the decision for them or rewrite their work. The goal is that they learn by working through it, with Chris as a sounding board.

**3. Review rigorously — for the right reasons.**
When code comes back, Chris checks four things:
- Is the PR description clear enough that someone six months from now understands the "why"?
- Is the code maintainable for the next person who touches it?
- Does it follow the patterns the team already agreed on?
- Is it correct?

### How Chris Delivers Feedback

Every piece of feedback comes with:
- A concrete example of what "good" looks like — not just what's wrong
- A pointer to the existing pattern or template, if one exists
- An open door — he makes clear he's available to talk it through
- The bigger picture — why this matters for the team, not just this one PR

> **In his own words:** "I see helping people advance as a core part of my job. I calibrate autonomy to where someone is, guide them through the hard decisions so they actually learn, and then review carefully — not to nitpick, but to make sure it's clear, maintainable, and sets them up for the next level. I'm always available to talk it through, and I connect it to the bigger picture so it doesn't feel arbitrary."

## What Chris Needs From a Manager

### Early, Honest Feedback — Not Surprises
Chris is senior enough that he doesn't need day-to-day direction. What he does need is to hear **early** if something isn't landing the way a manager hoped, so he can adjust course. The worst-case scenario is finding out late that expectations were different than he understood.

### Real Challenge, Not Status Quo
Chris wants projects and features substantial enough to actually demonstrate what he can do over time. Maintenance-only work with no growth trajectory is what he actively wants to avoid in his next role. He's at his best when the problem is genuinely hard.

### A Manager Who Absorbs Organizational Noise
Chris wants a manager who buffers the team from shifting priorities and organizational turbulence — so Chris and the team can stay focused on doing the actual work well, not navigating internal politics.
